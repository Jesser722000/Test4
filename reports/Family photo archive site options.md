# Build the Richards Family a Time Machine

The best way to give the Richards family (and later the Arants) a private, genuinely engaging archive is **Option 1, "Our own family time machine"**: a custom site on Cloudflare (static Astro pages, R2 storage, an emailed one-time-code login through Cloudflare Access, and a small D1 database for "Who is this?" answers) that costs about **$1–5 a month** to run and is the only option that meets every requirement. An [interactive preview of all five options](https://claude.ai/artifact/PAYpYg4QJcPJUo4hZJLQYe) lays them out side by side; it is private to you unless you share it. Underneath the site, the real archive is the scanning spreadsheet plus the master scans, with dates, names and stories written into the files and the masters backed up on Backblaze B2 or Cloudflare R2 for a few dollars a month. Immich, an open-source app that works like Google Photos, is an optional helper rather than a first step: running it on PikaPods for roughly **$7–10 a month** while you scan adds automatic face grouping and an early private look for relatives, and the names you give faces there can feed the custom site's person pages. Each alternative wins on only one axis. Immich on its own (Option 2) is the richest ready-made app but stores exact dates only; CollectionBuilder (Option 3) turns the spreadsheet into a museum-style catalogue but has no logins or contribution tools of its own; Gramps Web (Option 4) stores "about 1955" natively and ties faces to a family tree but leaves video undocumented; and shared albums on Ente, Google Photos or iCloud (Option 5) are the least work but offer a generic grid. What families and institutions report points one way—older relatives gravitate to whatever takes the least effort (a frame, a shared album, a group chat) and drift away when asked to create accounts ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=26013715))—so an easy login, browsing by decade and a sit-back TV mode matter more than feature counts. Every option rests on the same scanning habits—600 ppi lossless TIFF masters, scanned backs, file IDs based on where each print is kept, and two date fields per file—because among the mainstream photo apps reviewed only Flickr documents a way to store a "circa" date, and services such as Google Photos keep later edits outside the files. Several prices came from third-party trackers that disagree by as much as 50%, and no example site could be loaded directly to confirm it is still online, so treat specific figures as starting points to re-check before buying.

## Relatives engage when nothing stands between them and the photos

The research found no personal family-archive website with a documented record of what relatives actually used, so the design lessons come from builders' anecdotes, institutional projects and usability studies—and all three point the same way. Families on Hacker News describe grandparents delighted by Aura frames that show "a new pic of the kid almost every day" and by private Flickr albums ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35139430)), older relatives preferring a Telegram group as "super easy, fast," and photo books recommended for longevity ([Ask HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=29978099)). A privacy-first family site called Haven struggled because some relatives refused to create accounts ([HN: Show HN: Haven](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=26013715)). Usability research explains why. In Nielsen Norman Group studies, known here through secondhand summaries, users over 65 were **43% slower** than users aged 21–55 and failed more tasks ([MedCity News](https://medcitynews.com/2013/06/the-case-for-making-your-website-senior-friendly/)), and the W3C found that sites built to existing accessibility standards meet most older users' needs ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/older-users/)). WCAG 2.2, the current accessibility standard, makes that concrete: tap targets of at least 24×24 CSS pixels, and a single-tap alternative for anything done by dragging, such as timeline scrubbers, before-and-after sliders and maps ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)).

The engaging ideas come from institutions. **Fortepan**, a Hungarian archive built largely from snapshots donated by families, lets visitors move through 1900–1990 by year and decade ([Budapest Times](https://www.budapesttimes.hu/hungary/90-years-of-photos-to-browse/)) and now pins about 45,000 photos to a map that works well on phones ([BudAppest](https://budappest.com/budapest-just-got-a-time-machine-and-you-can-use-it-right-from-your-phone/)). The British Museum's Museum of the World places objects on one time axis split into parallel geographic "tracks" ([Internet Archaeology](https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue44/13)), a layout that maps directly onto a Richards track and an Arant track sharing the same years. Crowdsourced identification works at volume with little moderation: the Library of Congress's 2008 Flickr pilot drew **67,176 tags and 7,166 comments**, removed fewer than 25 contributions as inappropriate, and improved more than 500 catalog records ([librarian.net](https://www.librarian.net/stax/2607/library-of-congress-reports-on-flickr-project); [LoC](https://wwws.loc.gov/rr/print/flickr_pilot.html)), while ETH Library keeps volunteers coming back by posting new unidentified photos every week and celebrating the solved ones ([infoclio.ch](https://www.infoclio.ch/en/node/143458)). Civil War Photo Sleuth shows the right split for faces—software proposes candidates and people confirm them—and warns that bigger candidate pools produce more false matches ([IUI 2019 paper](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10139505-photo-sleuth-combining-human-expertise-face-recognition-identify-historical-portraits)), the same trap that look-alike siblings set in a family archive.

Two further lessons round out the picture. Facebook's 2014 Year in Review showed a father a photo of his daughter, who had died that June, an episode he called "inadvertent algorithmic cruelty" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2014-12-27-facebooks-year-in-review-shows-tragic-side-of-software.html)); it argues for resurfacing photos only from a hand-picked set, never automatically, and for "hide this person or date" controls like those Facebook later added ([SlashGear](https://www.slashgear.com/1682281/stop-memory-notifications-facebook/)). Rick Prelinger's "Lost Landscapes" screenings, where audiences call out memories over silent home movies ([KQED](https://www.kqed.org/arts/10179587/forgotten-footage-illuminates-san-franciscos-lost-landscapes)), suggest that the conversation around a home video is itself worth recording.

These findings give the yardstick for judging the options below: no account wall, or a login remembered for weeks; browsing by decade first, with uncertain dates labeled "circa" rather than hidden; a page for each person; home videos cut into short chapters that sit on the same timeline as the photos; contributions asked one question at a time; a sit-back TV or slideshow mode; controls for sensitive memories; and data that outlives the website. Dates deserve the most attention, because scanned prints rarely carry them, and as a FamilySearch guide warns, a scan without a record of who is pictured, when and where loses much of its value ([FamilySearch blog](https://familysearch.org/blog/en/common-mistake-scanning-family-photos)).

## Five ways to build it, from bespoke to rented

The five options are numbered to match the interactive preview, starting with the recommended custom build and ending with the lowest-effort hosted route. One point applies to all five. None of the reviewed services reliably writes edited dates and captions back into the files it exports; Google Takeout, for example, delivers dates, descriptions and album membership as separate JSON "sidecar" files next to each photo ([PixelUnion](https://pixelunion.eu/blog/how-to-keep-metadata-when-exporting-google-photos/)). The master files on your own drives, with metadata embedded before upload, are therefore the archive, and each option is a replaceable viewing layer on top. The cost estimates assume about 5,000 photos and 40 hours of tape, which the research team estimated at roughly 50 GB of web-quality copies; the masters are far larger.

### 1. Our own family time machine: a custom Cloudflare site that clears every bar

**What the family experiences.** A custom site can include every pattern on the yardstick. The home screen offers four large doors—Browse by year, Browse by person, Play a slideshow, Help us identify—and decade "chapters" lay a Richards track and an Arant track side by side on one year axis. A photo grid opens each photo full-size with a "Turn over" button that shows the scanned back and a typed transcription of its handwriting; an album view pages through the original album layout with big Previous and Next buttons; a map pins photos to the farm, the church and the old houses; and person pages show "Grandma through the years." Short video chapters sit on the timeline beside that year's photos, with captions and searchable transcripts. Relatives answer "Who is this?" with large buttons, including "I don't know," or record a voice note telling a photo's story, and a TV mode plays a decade on a loop with big captions and no surprise sound. Signing in means typing an email address and a one-time code, then staying signed in for up to a month.

**How it works.** The heavy media work runs once, on your computer, through an "ingest" script. For photos, sharp (an image-processing library) writes small, modern-format web copies at several sizes, each with a tiny blurred preview that shows while it loads ([sharp](https://github.com/lovell/sharp); [ThumbHash](https://github.com/evanw/thumbhash)). For video, the ffmpeg toolkit removes the interlacing flicker from tape transfers ([FFmpeg](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/filters.texi)), PySceneDetect splits long tapes at cuts and fades so you can review the chapter breaks ([PySceneDetect](https://github.com/Breakthrough/PySceneDetect)), and ffmpeg then encodes each chapter into HLS streaming files stored on R2 ([FFmpeg](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/muxers.texi)); if you would rather not encode, a streaming service such as Bunny Stream, Mux or Cloudflare Stream can take the clips instead. faster-whisper, a speech-to-text tool, writes the transcripts ([faster-whisper](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper)). The script uploads everything to R2 and saves a "manifest" listing every item and its details. A static site built with Astro ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/astro)) reads that list and rebuilds quickly because it does no image processing itself, while a small server-side program on Cloudflare Workers, with a D1 database, stores comments, "Who is this?" answers, stories and uploads. Dates are stored in EDTF, a standard notation for uncertain dates in which `1965~` means about 1965 and `196X` means sometime in the 1960s, and the edtf.js library converts these into earliest and latest possible dates for sorting ([edtf.js](https://github.com/inukshuk/edtf.js)). Each physical print is one item with front and back images, linked to people, places, source albums and family collections. The interface uses free, permissively licensed building blocks: PhotoSwipe for full-size viewing ([PhotoSwipe](https://www.npmjs.com/package/photoswipe)), StPageFlip for turning album pages ([StPageFlip](https://github.com/Nodlik/StPageFlip)), MapLibre for maps ([MapLibre](https://www.npmjs.com/package/maplibre-gl)), Topola for family-tree charts drawn from standard genealogy files ([Topola](https://github.com/PeWu/topola)), hls.js for video playback ([hls.js](https://www.npmjs.com/package/hls.js)) and Pagefind for search ([Pagefind](https://www.npmjs.com/package/pagefind)).

**Pros and cons.** This is the only option that combines honest approximate dates, contributions credited to the relative who made them, private media and a fully designed experience, and it is among the cheapest to run. The price is effort and ownership. Even with an AI coding assistant, my estimate is a few weekends for a first version (timeline, full-size viewer with backs, login) and months for the full feature list, and you become the maintainer of custom code that few others in the family could fix. Faces are the hardest piece to build yourself: the standard face-recognition models (InsightFace) are licensed for non-commercial research only, and Immich's permission to use them does not extend to anyone else ([InsightFace](https://github.com/deepinsight/insightface); [Immich](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/blob/main/machine-learning/README.md)). The practical path is to group and name faces in Immich itself, then copy them out to sidecar files with the community tool immich-export ([immich-export](https://github.com/peemot/immich-export)), which is why the recommendation keeps Immich as an optional helper. AI dating is coarse—the DEW research model is off by about **7.1 years** on average ([DEW-Model](https://github.com/TIB-Visual-Analytics/DEW-Model))—so machine estimates should be stored as suggestions, never as facts.

**Costs, setup and upkeep.** A lean setup costs **$1–5 a month**: at about 50 GB of web copies and self-encoded video, R2 storage runs about $0.60 a month after the free 10 GB, with no download fees, and the free tiers of Access, D1 and Workers easily cover a few dozen relatives ([R2](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/); [D1](https://developers.cloudflare.com/d1/platform/pricing/); [Workers](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)). Handing the video to a streaming service instead costs about $1–2 a month on Bunny Stream ([Bunny](https://bunny.net/docs/stream/pricing); [Bunny](https://bunny.net/pricing/stream/)), about $5.76 on Mux ([Mux](https://www.mux.com/docs/pricing/overview)) or about $17.40 on Cloudflare Stream for 40 hours of tape ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/stream/pricing/)), and a comfortable setup that also adds Workers' $5-a-month paid plan runs about $10–30 a month. Keeping 250 GB of masters in Backblaze B2 adds about $1.74 a month at $6.95 per TB ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/cloud-storage/pricing)). A one-time AI pass that drafts decade guesses, scene descriptions and transcriptions of the handwriting on backs for 10,000 front and back scans would cost roughly **$2 with Anthropic's current Haiku-class model in batch mode up to $140 with its current Opus-class model at standard rates**, assuming per-million-token prices of $0.10 in and $0.50 out for the Haiku class and $4 in and $20 out for the Opus class, a 50% batch discount, images shrunk to about 1,600 tokens each, and no thinking tokens ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Transcribing 40 hours of tape costs about $1.20 on Cloudflare's AI service, or nothing on your own computer ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers-ai/platform/pricing/)). Ongoing work means running the ingest script after scanning sessions, reviewing suggestions weekly and updating the site's software libraries now and then.

**Privacy, video, contributions and longevity.** Privacy is the strongest of the five, because every page, photo, video segment and database request sits behind Cloudflare Access's emailed one-time code, provided you avoid two setup traps. New Cloudflare Zero Trust accounts no longer turn on the one-time-code login automatically, so relatives without Cloudflare accounts are locked out until you add it ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/integrations/identity-providers/one-time-pin/)), and connecting your own domain to an R2 storage bucket before setting up its Access protection makes the bucket public ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/tutorials/cloudflare-access/)). Sign-ins can last up to a month ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/access-controls/access-settings/session-management/)), and because each relative holds one of the 50 free seats until removed, turn on automatic seat expiration ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/cloudflare-one/team-and-resources/users/seat-management/)). Cheaper-looking shortcuts fail: StaticCrypt encrypts only the web pages and leaves images and videos unprotected ([StaticCrypt](https://github.com/robinmoisson/staticrypt)), and Supabase's free tier pauses a project after a week without activity ([Supabase](https://github.com/supabase/supabase/blob/master/packages/shared-data/plans.ts)). Video support is the best of the five, with short streaming chapters, captions and transcripts, and contributions are the richest: relatives can suggest names, answer simple multiple-choice dating questions ("Before or after the wedding?"), add stories and voice notes, and upload photos through short-lived private links into a folder you review ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/api/s3/presigned-urls/)). Longevity splits in two. The static pages, the item list and the masters are as durable as Option 3, but the interactive features depend on Cloudflare's services, so export the database on a schedule, write accepted answers back into the master files, and keep a one-page handover note explaining how to rebuild the site.

### 2. A private Google Photos you own: Immich adds faces, maps and memories

**What the family experiences and how it works.** Immich looks and behaves like Google Photos, but it runs on a server you control, either your own hardware or managed hosting on PikaPods. Relatives scroll one timeline of photos and videos, browse a People page of named faces (with each person's age shown once you enter birth dates), explore a map, see "on this day" memories ([Immich](https://immich.app/features)) and, since October 2026, birthday memories that bring back a person's earlier birthdays ([GitHub v3.3.0](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases/tag/v3.3.0)), and search in plain language or for text that appears in a photo ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/features/searching.md)). Behind the scenes, a few Docker containers—the server, a machine-learning service and a Postgres database—detect faces and group them into people, creating a new person once three similar faces are found, and you then name, merge or hide each one ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/features/facial-recognition.md)). The project declared itself stable with v2.0 in October 2025 ([Immich blog](https://immich.app/blog/v2.0.0-release)), released v3.0 in July 2026, and reached v3.3.3 on October 14, 2026 ([GitHub](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases)). Relatives get in one of two ways: a shared album link, which needs no account and can be set to require a password, expire, and allow downloads or uploads ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/features/sharing.md)); or a login, which unlocks the full timeline, people, map and comments.

**Pros and cons.** It is the richest ready-made experience of the five. It reads dates, descriptions and tags from XMP sidecar files and writes edits made in the app to those sidecars instead of changing the originals ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/features/xmp-sidecars.md); [Immich FAQ](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/FAQ.mdx)), and since v3.3 a relative with an account can edit a shared person's name and birth date ([GitHub v3.3.0](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases/tag/v3.3.0)). The gaps matter for prints, though. Immich has **no approximate dates**, and its bulk date tool sets one exact date and cannot shift a group of dates by an offset, a hole that third-party add-ons such as immich-power-tools fill ([Immich discussion](https://git.tdem.in/immich-app/immich/discussions/9586); [BrightCoding](https://blog.brightcoding.dev/2026/09/24/immich-power-toolsimmich-power-tools-bulk-organize-your-self-hosted-immich-library)). "Stacking" can tuck a print's back under its front, but only in the main timeline; albums and other views list stacked items separately ([GitHub v1.83.0](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases/tag/v1.83.0)), so relatives viewing a shared album would likely see backs as loose photos. Comments and likes require a login and, according to archived documentation, are hidden from people who open a shared link ([Immich archived docs](https://v1.121.0.archive.immich.app/docs/features/shared-albums)). The project is also fast-moving and still maturing: on-the-fly video conversion for streaming is labeled experimental ([GitHub v3.0.0](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases/tag/v3.0.0)), and Immich's own guide says never to expose its port directly to the internet because "severe security vulnerabilities cannot be ruled out" ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/guides/remote-access.md)).

**Costs, setup and upkeep.** Managed hosting on PikaPods is the low-effort route, quoted "from $6.80/mo" in a competitor's comparison ([InstaPods](https://instapods.com/vs/pikapods/)); one user reported paying just under $10 a month for 175 GB ([micro.blog](https://micro.blog/rom/55606229)), or roughly **$80–120 a year**. Elestio starts around $11 a month and runs $16–17 for 2 virtual CPUs and 4 GB of memory ([itechguides](https://www.itechguides.com/products/elestio-immich/); [Elestio](https://elest.io/elestio-vs-fly-io)). Self-hosting takes at least 6 GB of memory (8 GB recommended) with the database on a local SSD ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/install/requirements.md)), which rules out Hetzner's 4 GB entry-level server, itself repriced upward in June 2026 ([Hetzner](https://docs.hetzner.com/general/infrastructure-and-availability/price-adjustment/)). A home mini PC or network storage box (NAS) costs more up front but little each month, though Synology's 2025 Plus models officially support only Synology-branded drives ([NASCompares](https://nascompares.com/2025/08/04/synology-ds225-nas-review/)). Optional supporter licenses cost $24.99 per user or $99.99 per server at 2024 prices ([Immich](https://v1.109.0.archive.immich.app/blog/2024/immich-licensing)). Setup takes an afternoon on PikaPods or a weekend at home. Upkeep means applying updates about monthly after reading the release notes, and keeping your own backup copies of the photos, because Immich's automatic daily database backups contain no photos or videos ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/administration/backup-and-restore.md)).

**Privacy, video, contributions and longevity.** Password-protected links and accounts keep it family-only. A home server should sit behind Cloudflare Tunnel or a reverse proxy rather than being opened to the internet directly, but Cloudflare's free and Pro plans cap each upload at 100 MB, so long video files have to be uploaded from inside the house ([Immich discussion](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/discussions/13573)). Videos sit on the timeline alongside photos and play in a new web player, and casting to a Chromecast exists but is experimental and needs the server reachable at a public HTTPS address ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/features/casting.md)). Relatives with accounts can comment, name faces and add photos to albums they are allowed to edit, while link visitors can only view, download or upload. One shortcut for elders is a single shared "family" login that receives your whole library through "partner sharing"; partner sharing leaves out face data ([Immich docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/immich-app/immich/main/docs/docs/features/partner-sharing.md)), but v3.3's shared people bring the names back, at the cost of no longer knowing which relative said what. Plan for the Arants before naming anyone, because v3.2's "cluster groups" recognize faces across several trusted accounts, but switching them on resets face recognition and clears all names and birth dates ([GitHub v3.2.0](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases/tag/v3.2.0)). The software is open source under the AGPL license ([Immich license](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/blob/main/LICENSE)) and actively developed; an instance survives only as long as someone pays for and updates it, but the original files outlive it. Run alongside Option 1, the same setup becomes the optional face-naming helper described in the recommendation.

### 3. A digital family museum: CollectionBuilder turns the spreadsheet into a catalogue

**What the family experiences and how it works.** The result feels like a museum's online collection: a home page plus Browse, Subjects (a word cloud), Map, Timeline and Data pages, and a page per item with the image or video, its details and a download button ([UTK workshop](https://utk-collection-builder.readthedocs.io/en/latest/contents/1_outline.html)). CollectionBuilder-CSV, a website template from the University of Idaho Library ([CollectionBuilder](https://collectionbuilder.github.io/about.html)), generates all of it from one spreadsheet with a row per item plus a folder of files. Each row's location field can point to a local file or a web address ([CB docs](https://collectionbuilder.github.io/cb-docs/docs/metadata/object-paths/)), so images and videos can sit in Cloudflare's R2 storage behind the same login, and MP4 clips you host yourself play in a standard web video player with a preview image ([GitHub](https://github.com/CollectionBuilder/collectionbuilder-csv/blob/main/_includes/item/video-player.html)). GitHub rebuilds the site automatically and a static host serves it; family-only access comes from putting the whole site behind Cloudflare Access, where relatives enter their email address and a one-time code.

**Pros and cons.** The spreadsheet you should keep anyway becomes the website—a habit the recommended plan borrows even if you build Option 1 instead—the output is plain web pages with no server to patch, and the template is actively developed (its last change landed October 8, 2026), though it publishes no formal releases ([GitHub](https://github.com/CollectionBuilder/collectionbuilder-csv)). Against that, it has no logins, comments or contribution tools of its own, no face recognition, and a library-catalogue look rather than a story; its timeline needs exact year-month-day dates, so uncertain dates go in a separate display field ([UTK workshop](https://utk-collection-builder.readthedocs.io/en/latest/contents/1_outline.html)); and no published example built from family photos was found. One shortcut is a privacy trap: the CollectionBuilder-Sheets variant reads its data from a Google Sheet published to the web ([GitHub](https://github.com/CollectionBuilder/collectionbuilder-sheets)), which exposes names and captions to anyone with the link.

**Costs, setup and upkeep.** The software is free under the MIT license, and Cloudflare's pieces are free or nearly so: Access is free for up to 50 users ([Cloudflare](https://en-us.www.cloudflare.com/teams-pricing/)); Pages hosting is free within limits of 20,000 files per site and 25 MiB per file, which pushes media into R2 ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/pages/platform/limits/)); and R2 charges $0.015 per GB per month beyond 10 GB free, with no download fees ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)). That puts the archive at about **$1–5 a month** plus a domain name. Netlify costs more for the same result, because its site password requires the Pro plan, from $20 a month ([Netlify](https://docs.netlify.com/manage/security/secure-access-to-sites/password-protection/)). Expect a weekend or two of setup with an AI coding assistant; upkeep means adding rows, regenerating thumbnails and rebuilding after each scanning batch.

**Privacy, video, contributions and longevity.** Privacy is only as strong as the host's settings, but Cloudflare Access is strong as long as it also covers the address the photos and videos are served from (setup details under Option 1). Video works as files you host yourself; embedding YouTube or Vimeo would hand privacy to those platforms. Relatives contribute names, dates and stories indirectly, by email or a form, and you fold them into the spreadsheet. Longevity is the best of any website here, because static web pages and a spreadsheet file can be copied to any host or even a USB stick. The bigger alternative is **Omeka S** (v4.2.1, June 2026), worth choosing if family members need their own logins to add items through web forms: it offers private sites, a "Viewer" role and a maintained Mapping module whose timeline steps through items in date order ([GitHub](https://github.com/omeka/omeka-s/releases); [Omeka forum](https://forum.omeka.org/t/restrict-acces-to-omeka-site/27277); [Omeka manual](https://omeka.org/s/docs/user-manual/modules/mapping/)), but it is a server you must keep patched, and the hosted Omeka.net service offers only 2–50 GB for $35–1,000 a year ([Omeka.net](https://www.omeka.net/signup)). At the other extreme, **sigal** and **thumbsup** turn folders into static galleries sorted by each file's embedded date and convert video for the web ([sigal](https://github.com/saimn/sigal); [thumbsup](https://github.com/thumbsup/thumbsup)), which works only if the real dates were written into the files first, since a fresh scan carries the day it was scanned.

### 4. Family tree first: Gramps Web makes the tree the index and the dates honest

**What the family experiences and how it works.** Relatives sign in and navigate by people: ancestor, descendant, fan and hourglass charts; person pages with their photos; interactive maps with historical overlays; and a blog of stories with images ([Gramps Web](https://github.com/gramps-project/gramps-web)). Family historians with editing rights click the dashed boxes that face detection draws on a photo and link each face to someone in the tree ([Gramps Web docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gramps-project/gramps-web-docs/main/docs/en/user-guide/tagging.md)), and since v26.10.1 (October 11, 2026) marked areas of an image can also link to families, events and places ([GitHub](https://github.com/gramps-project/gramps-web/releases)). Each photo carries its own date in Gramps' genealogy date format, which supports "about," "before," "after," ranges, "estimated" and year-only values ([Gramps](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gramps-project/gramps/master/gramps/gen/lib/media.py); [Gramps](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gramps-project/gramps/master/gramps/gen/lib/date.py)).

**Pros and cons.** It answers "who is this, how are they related, and roughly when" better than any photo app, and the tree is the natural place for the Arant family to join the Richards. But it is a genealogy database first: the research found no date-ordered view of all photos and videos, no memories feature and no documentation of video handling. Tagging requires editing rights, so older relatives would browse while one or two family historians do the identifying, and family trees "don't mix well with small screens," as one builder put it ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44121733)). Pairing it with a photo app means entering people and dates twice, and the desktop version of Gramps cannot read face markings already saved inside image files ([Gramps forum](https://gramps.discourse.group/t/suggested-update-to-phototagginggramplet-to-support-xmp-face-regions/1209)).

**Costs, setup and upkeep.** Grampshub hosting costs €4 a month for trees of up to 500 people and €10 for up to 10,000, roughly $4.60–11.60, after a 14-day trial ([Grampshub](https://www.grampshub.com/); [Gramps forum](https://gramps.discourse.group/t/gramps-web-maximum-size/6973)), and its founder has said there will be no paid premium features ([Gramps forum](https://gramps.discourse.group/t/announcing-grampshub-a-hosting-provider-for-gramps-web/3838)). A one-click install on DigitalOcean starts at $6 a month ([Gramps forum](https://gramps.discourse.group/t/gramps-web-digitalocean-1-click-app/2733)), and self-hosting needs about 2 GB of memory ([Gramps Web](https://www.grampsweb.org/install_setup/digital_ocean/)). Setup takes an afternoon; building the tree is the real work. Upkeep on Grampshub is minimal, with new releases every few weeks.

**Privacy, video, contributions and longevity.** Access is by login only, with owner, editor, contributor and member roles ([Gramps Web](https://github.com/gramps-project/gramps-web)). Video would have to be hosted somewhere else. Relatives contribute by tagging faces, editing the tree together and writing blog stories. Longevity is good because the tree can be exported as GEDCOM, the standard genealogy file format, which FamilySearch develops in the open ([FamilySearch](https://github.com/FamilySearch/GEDCOM)), although Grampshub's limits on photo storage were not found.

**Hosted alternatives.** The big genealogy sites fit a private archive less well. **FamilySearch Memories** is free and stores dates such as "about 1944" ([FamilySearch Community](https://community.familysearch.org/en/discussion/185006/photos-with-unknown-exact-dates)), but it takes no video and caps files at 15 MB ([FamilySearch Help](https://www.familysearch.org/en/help/helpcenter/article/how-many-memories-photos-stories-documents-and-audio-files-can-i-upload)), and its help pages describe memories of deceased people as public, while contradicting each other on the other defaults ([FamilySearch Help](https://www.familysearch.org/en/help/helpcenter/article/who-can-view-the-items-uploaded-to-memories); [FamilySearch Help](https://www.familysearch.org/en/help/helpcenter/article/can-i-add-memories-about-my-living-relatives-to-family-tree)), so treat it as a public layer rather than a private archive. **MyHeritage** has the most complete set of AI tools for old photos among the services reviewed—animation, colorization, enhancement and the free PhotoDater—but most of them need a genealogy subscription, with free use watermarked or limited ([Magic Memory](https://magic-memory.dev/alternatives/myheritage); [The Family Heart](https://thefamilyheart.com/myheritage-in-color/)); unverified third-party listings put plans at $119–299 a year ([Magic Memory](https://magic-memory.dev/alternatives/myheritage); [Subger](https://subger.com/ja/service/myheritage)), and an older source gives a 50 MB video limit ([Family Tree Magazine](https://familytreemagazine.com/websites/online-tools-sharing-family-photos-stories/)). **Ancestry** caps uploads at 15 MB ([Ancestry Help](https://help.ancestry.com/hc/en-us/articles/53933347909651-Uploading-Media-Photos-Stories-Audio-and-Documents)).

### 5. Shared albums plus a story timeline: hosted services get relatives browsing this week

**What the family experiences and how it works.** Relatives tap a link and scroll a grid of photos and videos sorted by date, exactly like a phone gallery. You upload finished JPEG viewing copies and MP4 clips with dates already embedded, group them into decade or event albums, and share each one, optionally adding a story-style TimelineJS page of highlights (described below). Three services fit best. **Ente** is the privacy pick: its storage is end-to-end encrypted ([Ente blog](https://community.ente.io/blog/legacy)), and its album links can require a password, expire on a set date and optionally accept relatives' own photos, with no account needed to view ([Ente Help](https://ente.com/help/photos/features/sharing-and-collaboration/public-links)). **Google Photos** is the convenience pick for TV: anyone with an album link can view without a Google account, though adding photos requires one ([Guiding Tech](https://www.guidingtech.com/google-photos-link-sharing-permissions-settings-guide/)), and Google TV, Chromecast and Nest Hub devices can play chosen albums as a background slideshow ([Google Photos Help](https://support.google.com/photos/answer/16996534)), the nearest thing to a digital photo frame in any option here. **iCloud** is the convenience pick for an Apple family: a Shared Album holds up to 100 subscribers and 5,000 items, and an optional public-website link lets anyone with the link view without an Apple login ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/148868); [iPhone Life](https://iphonelife.com/node/263691)). A third-party blog says Apple TV's Photos app can show shared albums, but Apple's own documentation on that was not found ([PicBackMan](https://www.picbackman.com/tips-tricks/how-to-share-photos-on-your-iphone-with-icloud-photo-sharing/)).

**Pros and cons.** There is nothing to install or patch, storage is cheap, and Google's TV support is unmatched, but the weaknesses are built in. The experience is a generic grid rather than a story. No source showed Google Photos, Apple Photos, Amazon Photos or Ente storing approximate dates, so "circa 1965" has to be faked with a placeholder date plus a caption; Flickr is the exception, with a date-taken field that can hold a year and month, a year only, or "circa" ([Flickr API](https://www.flickr.com/services/api/misc.dates.html)). In Google Photos, the names you give faces are visible only to the account that added them and are not shared with an album ([Google Photos Help](https://support.google.com/photos/answer/6128838?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop)), so Grandma's identifications would never reach the cousins. Google's web app can set one date across many selected photos ([Labnol](https://www.labnol.org/internet/change-google-photos-date-time/29008)), but forum users report that it ignores caption changes made to a file after upload ([ExifTool forum](https://exiftool.org/forum/index.php?topic=8536.0)). Apple's Mac Photos app applies a batch date change only as an offset and cannot set many photos to one date ([MacMost](https://macmost.com/how-to-change-photo-time-and-location-in-mac-photos.html); [Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/254871096)), and iCloud shared albums are viewing copies only: they shrink photos to 2,048 pixels on the long edge ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/148868)), and an April 2026 forum thread reports heavily downscaled videos and originals that cannot be retrieved ([Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/256274936)). Apple's six-person Shared Photo Library is a different product that requires an Apple Account for each member ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/guide/icloud-shared-photo-library); [iBoysoft](https://iboysoft.com/wiki/icloud-shared-photo-library.html)). Amazon Photos fits worst: scans without an embedded date show as "no date" ([ExifTool forum](https://exiftool.org/forum/index.php?msg=66392)), and Prime includes only 5 GB of video ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Amazon-Photos/b?ie=UTF8&node=13234696011)).

**Costs, setup and upkeep.** Google One normally costs $19.99 a year for 100 GB and $99.99 for 2 TB ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/12/23/google-one-2026-offer/)). Ente lists $59.88 a year for 200 GB and $119.88 for 1 TB ([SammyGuru](https://sammyguru.com/ente-photos-review/)), though other 2026 reviews quote different figures ([Doolpa](https://doolpa.com/article/ente)), and one Ente plan can share its storage with up to five family members ([Ente Help](https://ente.com/help/photos/features/account/)). iCloud+ costs $2.99 a month for 200 GB or $9.99 for 2 TB, with no annual billing ([Internxt](https://blog.internxt.com/apple-icloud-pricing/)); shared-album subscribers pay nothing, though originals count against the album owner's storage ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/148868)). Because only viewing copies go up, a realistic budget is **$20–120 a year**. Setup takes an afternoon plus upload time, and upkeep is close to zero.

**Privacy, video, contributions and longevity.** Only Ente's links combine a password with an expiry date. Google's album links can have neither ([Viallo](https://www.viallo.app/blog/share-google-photos-album)), and iCloud's public-website links can be forwarded to anyone ([Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/254969495)), so both are unlisted rather than private. On video, secondary sources put Google's cap at about 10 GB per file ([Cloudspress](https://www.cloudspress.com/?p=618223)), enough for a full tape, although a September 2026 vendor comparison says Google publishes no limit at all ([Viallo](https://www.viallo.app/blog/shared-album-size-limits-compared)), and Ente's limits could not be found. The alternatives are tighter—Apple's Shared Albums cap videos at 15 minutes and play them at 720p ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/148868)), Flickr Pro allows 10 minutes and 1 GB ([AllPlatforms](https://allplatforms.io/flickr/)), and SmugMug 20 minutes at 1080p ([SmugMug Help](https://www.smugmughelp.com/hc/en-us/articles/18212983748628-Prepare-and-format-videos-for-upload))—which works only if tapes are cut into short chapters. Contributions are thin: Google shared albums allow comments and likes, on by default according to an older fan-run guide ([Picasa resources](https://www.sites.google.com/site/picasaresources/google-photos-1/albums-in-google-photos)), and Ente links can accept uploads, but names have to live in captions you maintain. Longevity rides on the vendor's product decisions. Google stopped automatic photo backup from Drive for desktop on August 10, 2026 ([Daily Mirror](https://www.dailymirror.lk/breaking-news/Google-Drive-is-ending-Google-Photos-backup-starting-August-10/108-347825)), and Amazon cancelled its PhotosPlus plan in September 2024 ([PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/amazon-cancels-photosplus_id161675)). Ente, a smaller company, offsets its higher risk with open-source code that includes the server ([Ente docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ente-io/ente/main/docs/docs/self-hosting/index.md)), an export that writes Takeout-style JSON files beside untouched originals ([Ente help](https://help.ente.io/photos/features/backup-and-sync/export)), and a Legacy feature that lets trusted contacts recover the account ([Ente blog](https://community.ente.io/blog/legacy)).

**The optional story timeline.** Knight Lab's open-source TimelineJS builds an interactive timeline from a spreadsheet ([York U](https://pressbooks.library.yorku.ca/csiw-workshops/chapter/about-the-knight-labs-tools)), which makes it a quick way to lay a few dozen milestone photos and clips, each with a short story, along the years. It accepts direct links to .mp4 files as well as YouTube and Vimeo page addresses ([Knight Lab](https://timeline.knightlab.com/docs/media-types.html)), and its code repository was updated as recently as September 22, 2026 ([GitHub](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/TimelineJS3)). The catch is privacy: TimelineJS cannot read its Google Sheet until the sheet is **published to the web** ([Knight Lab](https://timeline.knightlab.com)), so the names, dates and captions in it are readable by anyone with the link, and Knight Lab warns never to set the sheet to "anyone with the link can edit." Keep that timeline to material the family is comfortable making public, or switch to TimelineJS's own JSON format ([GitHub](https://github.com/NUKnightLab/TimelineJS)) with the files hosted behind a login, as a community template shows ([GitHub](https://github.com/amazighdotmobi/timelinejs-selfhosted)), which moves you toward Option 3 or Option 1.

Reading across the rows, three patterns stand out. Engagement rises with building effort, but running cost does not: the most engaging option, Option 1, is among the cheapest to run, because static pages and media stored in R2 need no server and carry no download fees, so its real price is your time and the risk that only you understand the code. No single ready-made product covers everything, either. The tools that handle video and engagement well (Options 2 and 5) store only exact dates and keep face names per account, while the tools that handle uncertain dates and family relationships (Option 4 and FamilySearch) handle video poorly or not at all; only a custom design brings the two together, with uncertain dates that record where each came from, prints with front and back sides, and people and family collections as their own records. Finally, longevity tracks how close the site is to plain files. Option 3's static output and Option 1's item list can be copied anywhere, open-source servers (Options 2 and 4) last as long as someone maintains them, and hosted services (Option 5) last as long as the vendor keeps the product and you keep paying.

### Recommended: Option 1, with Immich as an optional helper

Build **Option 1, "Our own family time machine,"** and treat the files underneath it as the real archive. That foundation borrows Option 3's metadata habit: one scanning spreadsheet with a row per item, plus TIFF photo masters and lossless (FFV1) video masters with dates, names, captions and IDs written into the files or their sidecar files. Back the masters up off-site on Backblaze B2, about $1.74 a month for 250 GB ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/cloud-storage/pricing)), or on R2's Infrequent Access tier, about $2.50 a month for the same amount plus $0.01 per GB read back and a 30-day minimum ([R2](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)). Then build the site in stages over the winter for about $1–5 a month: the decade timeline and the full-size viewer with backs first, then video chapters (self-encoded HLS on R2, or Bunny, Mux or Stream if you would rather not encode), person pages, the "Who is this?" queue in D1 and the TV mode. ExifTool, a free command-line tool, writes your dates and captions into the files, the site's ingest script reads them into its item list, and answers you accept from relatives are written back into the master files, so nothing important lives only in a database. All told, the plan runs about $3–8 a month plus a domain name, before any optional extras.

**Immich (Option 2) is an optional helper, not the first step.** Running it on PikaPods while you scan costs about **$7–10 a month (roughly $80–120 a year)**, plus the monthly updates any Immich server needs, which would bring the whole plan to roughly $10–18 a month. In return it adds three things Option 1 lacks at first. It groups faces automatically, which Option 1 cannot easily copy because of the face-recognition license terms described under Option 1. It gives relatives an early, private look through password-protected decade albums while Option 1 is still being built, which also shows you what they actually use. And the names you give faces there can feed Option 1's person pages, because immich-export writes them out to sidecar files that the ingest script reads. Skip it, and you lose only those conveniences: Option 1 still works, with faces named by hand or through relatives' answers. If you do run it, decide on cluster groups before naming anyone, and once Option 1's person pages are live, either keep Immich as the family's phone-app view or move it to your own computer and drop the hosting bill.

Change the plan if your priorities differ. If you would rather not maintain code, Option 2 on its own is still a good outcome, and Option 3 behind the same Cloudflare Access setup adds a durable catalogue. If privacy outranks everything and nobody will run software, choose Ente under Option 5. If a relative wants to own the genealogy, add Option 4 on Grampshub and export its GEDCOM file to draw Option 1's family-tree charts. For the grandparents' living room, Option 1's TV mode runs in a smart TV's web browser, and Google TV's ambient mode can show Google Photos albums ([Google Photos Help](https://support.google.com/photos/answer/16996534)), though whether that works with albums shared from another account was not verified. For elders who will never type, a story service such as Remento records spoken answers to weekly prompts and prints a book whose QR codes play the original recordings ([Remento](https://www.remento.co/journal/the-best-memory-book-for-family-stories)), for $99 for the first year before it renews automatically ([Remento](https://www.remento.co/remento-vs-storyworth)). Avoid StoryCorps for family-only stories, because published interviews go to the Library of Congress regardless of privacy settings ([StoryCorps Help](https://support.storycorps.org/help-center/if-i-record-an-interview-with-the-storycorps-app-or-storycorps-connect-does-that-mean-anyone-around-the-world-can-listen-to-it)).

## Scanning choices made today set every option's ceiling

Every option above can only be as good as the scans and their details, so the habits below pay off whichever site you build.

### Scan at 600 ppi to lossless TIFF, and capture every back

Federal standards set the minimum. The National Archives' 2023 digitization rule requires at least 400 ppi, at 8 or 16 bits per color channel, for photographic prints ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-36/chapter-XII/subchapter-B/part-1236/subpart-E/section-1236.50)). The federal digitization guidelines (FADGI) rate scans from one to four stars, and the University of Illinois Library reads the four-star level as 600 ppi for photographs ([Illinois](https://exhibits.library.illinois.edu/s/illinois-digitization/page/FADGIpractice)), although a 2024 white paper on the guidelines suggests 400 ppi can be enough ([FBC/QAI](https://www.fbcinc.com/source/virtualhall_images/2024_Virtual_Events/USDA_Innovation/QAI/AsktheExpert_FADGI_whitepaper_061124.pdf)). A 2025 guide to archiving personal collections, distributed by Sno-Isle Libraries, recommends 600 ppi for standard photos, 600–1,200 ppi for prints you might enlarge, and 1,200 ppi or more for slides and negatives ([Sno-Isle](https://www.sno-isle.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/90/2025/10/2025_personal-digital-archiving_quick-reference-guide.docx)), while the Library of Congress's familiar 300 ppi advice dates from 2013 ([LoC](https://blogs.loc.gov/thesignal/2013/07/you-say-you-want-a-resolution-how-many-dpippi-is-too-much/)). Save masters as TIFF files, either uncompressed or with lossless (LZW or ZIP) compression, and make JPEGs only as viewing copies ([Rocky Nook](https://rockynook.com/article/scanning-photos/)). A 2022 draft of the federal guidelines calls for scanning black-and-white originals in 16-bit color ([FADGI draft](https://www.digitizationguidelines.gov/guidelines/DRAFT%20Technical%20Guidelines%20for%20Digitizing%20Cultural%20Heritage%20Materials%20-%203rd%20Edition.pdf)), which keeps sepia toning, stains and colored ink on the backs; simpler guides accept 8-bit grayscale ([SI Archives](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/you-asked-we-answered-taking-care-your-own-archives)), so treat color as the safer default rather than a requirement. For 35mm film, practitioners work at 3,000–4,000 ppi and call anything higher "probably a waste" ([Each Moment](https://www.eachmoment.co.uk/blog/2-000-35mm-slides-scanned-resolution-file-sizes-and-what-a-real-carousel-looks)).

| Original | Resolution | Color and bit depth | Equipment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4×6 and 5×7 prints | 600 ppi (a 4×6 becomes 2,400×3,600 pixels, about 26 MB as an 8-bit TIFF) | Color, 8 bits per channel; 16 for badly faded color | FastFoto for sturdy loose prints |
| 8×10 prints | 400–600 ppi | Same | Flatbed, or FastFoto within its limits |
| Wallet-size and smaller | 1,200 ppi true optical | Same | Flatbed only (the FastFoto's 1,200 is interpolated, not true detail) |
| Black-and-white prints | As above | Color preferred over grayscale | Either |
| 35mm slides and negatives | 3,000–4,000 ppi | 16 bits per channel | Film-capable flatbed or a service |
| Album pages | A whole-page scan for context, before removing any photos | Color | Flatbed, or a phone app for fragile albums |

The Epson FastFoto FF-680W costs about $530 new from Epson or $400 refurbished, and no successor model was found ([Epson](https://epson.com/For-Home/Scanners/Photo-Scanners/FastFoto-FF-680W-Wireless-High-speed-Photo-Scanning-System/p/B11B237201); [Epson ReNew](https://epson.com/Certified-ReNew/Scanners/FastFoto-FF-680W-Wireless-High-speed-Photo-Scanning-System---Certified-ReNew/p/B11B237201-N)). It captures real detail at up to 600 dpi, while its 1,200 dpi setting is interpolated, meaning the software invents the extra pixels ([B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1424870-REG/epson_b11b237201_fastfoto_ff_680w_photo_scanner.html)); its fast 300 dpi mode gives only about 2.2 megapixels from a 4×6 print, below both federal minimums, so keep it for quick reference passes. Epson's manual says to wipe photos and clean the rollers every 300 scans or each session, and warns that rooms below about 59°F make prints curl ([Epson manual](https://files.support.epson.com/docid/cpd5/cpd55191.pdf)); an Epson representative has said the feed rollers "may leave track marks" ([Best Buy Q&A](https://www.bestbuy.com/site/questions/epson-fastfoto-ff-680w-wireless-high-speed-photo-scanning-system-black/6280709/question/55caf264-003f-3efd-bb6e-89c8f29976dd)). Test 20 prints you don't care about, inspect them at full zoom, and keep one-of-a-kind glossy prints out of the feeder. Wallet-size and smaller prints, cracked or curled prints, photos mounted on card, Polaroids, anything thicker than the feeder's limit of roughly 0.3 mm, album pages and film belong on a flatbed scanner. Epson's V600 and V850 flatbeds are reportedly discontinued, with the V850 Pro listed at $1,999 and used V600s selling for about $325–500 ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/articles/product-review-epson-v600-vs-v850-photo-scanners/); [Epson](https://epson.com/For-Work/Scanners/Photo-and-Graphics/Epson-Perfection-V850-Pro-Photo-Scanner/p/B11B224201); [eBay](https://www.ebay.com/shop/epson-perfection-v600?_nkw=epson+perfection+v600)). Phone apps are for relatives who won't lend their originals: Google PhotoScan has not been updated since 2023 ([AppBrain](https://www.appbrain.com/app/photoscan-by-google-photos/com.google.android.apps.photos.scanner)), and Photomyne costs about $60 a year ([CyberGuy](https://cyberguy.com/how-to/how-to-digitize-old-photos-slides/amp/)).

Scan every back, not only the ones with writing. Backs carry handwritten captions, photo-lab stamps and the paper maker's printed marks, which can date a print even when nothing is written on it ([TimelineScan](https://timelinescan.com/how-to-date-old-photos/)). The FastFoto saves the untouched front with no suffix, an automatically enhanced copy as `_a` and the back as `_b` ([Geauga County library](https://geaugalibrary.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Auto-Feeder-Scanner-Written-Tutorial-9.2025.pdf)), but by default it creates the `_b` file only "if something is detected" ([Monroe County library](https://www.mcdl.info/sites/default/files/migrated/FHLC_FastFoto.pdf)), and the suffixes can't be changed ([PhotoStructure forum](https://forum.photostructure.com/t/filename-based-file-aggregation-for-epson-fastfoto-enhanced-and-reverse-side-photos/1593)); look for a setting that always captures the back, and treat the `_a` files as disposable copies. Keep the untouched scan as the master for restoration too, because AI restoration can produce a face that is "statistically plausible but historically uncertain" ([EnhancerFree](https://enhancerfree.com/blog/why-ai-photo-restoration-changes-faces)). Label every restored or colorized copy, which the IPTC photo-metadata standard now supports with a value for marking AI-generated images ([IPTC](https://iptc.org/news/iptc-releases-draft-of-digital-source-type-vocabulary-to-support-synthetic-media/)). Infrared dust removal (Digital ICE) works only on transparent film, not on prints or on traditional silver black-and-white film ([Wikipedia: Digital ICE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_ICE)).

### Name files by where the print lives, never by what it shows

Name each file by where the physical item is kept, not by what it shows, because names and dates will change as relatives weigh in. An archiving guide recommends a storage-location code plus a sequence number, with the same reference also written into the image's description ([Camera-Digitize-Archive](https://camera-digitize-archive.com/2025/09/19/camera-scanning-archive-structure-priorities/)). For this archive, `RICH-B01-A03-P012-04.tif` is box 01, album 03, page 12, fourth photo (counting left to right and top to bottom); `-00` in place of the photo number marks the whole-page scan; `_b` marks a back; `RICH-B02-E05-0137.tif` is a loose print from envelope 05 in box 02; and an `ARAN-` prefix begins when the Arant boxes arrive. Use only letters, numbers, hyphens and underscores ([App Initio](https://appinitio.medium.com/naming-conventions-for-scanned-photos-45a7fd2eb681)), write the scheme down and store it with the originals ([Lightroom Queen forum](https://www.lightroomqueen.com/community/threads/naming-convention-for-scanned-images.39996/)), and label each physical box, envelope and album with the same ID. Scan every album page whole before scanning or removing its photos, since the layout and handwritten captions carry meaning and let a site offer an album view, and keep photo-lab envelopes together, since prints from one roll of film usually share a date.

### Write two dates into every file: a sortable guess and the honest one

Photos store dates in two standard places. The EXIF date field must include all six parts (year, month, day, hour, minute and second), while the XMP date field can hold just a year, or a year and month ([ExifTool FAQ](https://exiftool.org/faq.html)). So write two dates: a sortable best guess in EXIF "date taken" (DateTimeOriginal), such as `1965:07:01 12:00:00` for "circa 1965," and the honest precision in XMP date created (photoshop:DateCreated), such as `1965` or `1972-07`. Avoid zeros as placeholders, which cause "Month 00 out of range" errors ([ExifTool forum](https://exiftool.org/forum/index.php?topic=12518.0)). The ExifTool community fills unknown parts with the earliest possible moment, such as January 1 for a known year ([ExifTool forum](https://exiftool.org/forum/index.php?topic=11575.0)); a mid-year date splits the possible error instead, and either works if you write the rule down and stay consistent. Use noon so time-zone handling can't push the date onto a different day, and use the seconds to keep album order among photos that share an estimate, since Immich now sorts photos with the same timestamp by filename ([GitHub v3.2.0](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/releases/tag/v3.2.0)).

Then fill in the rest of each file's details. Put the dating evidence in the caption ("circa 1965 — square Instamatic print, A KODAK PAPER backprint; Jim, born 1960, looks about five") and add a date-quality keyword (`date-exact`, `date-lab-stamp` or `date-estimated`). Record who appears in the standard IPTC "Person Shown" field, along with each face's position in the image ([IPTC](https://www.iptc.org/std/photometadata/documentation/userguide/)); the free photo manager digiKam can write face positions and Immich can import them, though that import has open bugs in v3.2 ([Immich PR](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/pull/6455); [Immich issue](https://github.com/immich-app/immich/issues/31544)). Set the IPTC Digital Source Type to `print` or `negativeFilm` ([IPTC NewsCodes](https://cv.iptc.org/newscodes/digitalsourcetype/)). A short ExifTool script driven by the scanning spreadsheet can write all of this in bulk, but before processing thousands of files, upload five fully tagged test images to each site you're considering and check what displays and how they sort.

### Date undated prints by stacking two or three independent clues

Most undated prints can be placed within a decade by combining two or three independent clues ([TimelineScan](https://timelinescan.com/how-to-date-old-photos/)). Print formats give firm "no earlier than" dates: Kodak's square 126 film arrived with the Instamatic camera in 1963 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/126_film)) and 110 film in 1972 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/110_film)), Polaroid SX-70 instant prints with their thick bottom edge date from 1972 on, and borderless glossy 3½×5 and 4×6 prints point to the late 1970s through the 1990s ([TimelineScan](https://timelinescan.com/how-to-date-old-photos/)). Dates machine-printed on the border record when the print was developed, not when the picture was taken ([Maureen Taylor](https://maureentaylor.com/learn/how-to-date-an-old-photograph/)). The paper maker's marks on the back narrow the era—one commercial guide dates "A KODAK PAPER" to about 1961–1972 and "THIS PAPER MANUFACTURED BY KODAK" to mid-1972 through early 1991 ([TimelineScan](https://timelinescan.com/how-to-date-old-photos/))—though other guides give conflicting ranges ([The Photo Managers](https://thephotomanagers.com/photo-detective-dating-family-snapshots/)). Wavy (deckled) edges suggest the 1930s to 1950s but are weak evidence, because Kodak sold pre-cut deckled paper into the late 1960s or early 1970s ([Dickerman Prints](https://www.dickermanprints.com/why-do-old-time-photos-have-jagged-edges/)). Clothing, hair and cars narrow the decade, since skirts got shorter through most of the twentieth century ([Family Tree Magazine](https://familytreemagazine.com/premium/uncategorized/dating-modern-photos/)), and children whose birth year you know give the tightest clue of all. Handwritten captions deserve skepticism, because you rarely know who wrote them or when ([Family Locket](https://familylocket.com/rlp-120-identifying-and-preserving-photographs-with-maureen-taylor/)).

AI helps only as a first guess. MyHeritage's free PhotoDater covers photos from 1860 to 1990 and puts about 60% of its estimates within five years ([MyHeritage](https://blog.myheritage.com/2023/08/introducing-photodater-an-exclusive-free-new-feature-to-estimate-when-old-photos-were-taken/)), and in PetaPixel's test it dated a 1942 photo to 1947 ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2023/11/16/this-ai-tool-will-estimate-when-an-old-photo-was-taken/)), so record its answer as one clue among several ("PhotoDater: 1968 ±5"). Let relatives settle the dates at small "photo detective" sessions that work through one album or box at a time, with cousins bringing their own albums ([The Photo Managers](https://thephotomanagers.com/dating-identifying-old-family-photographs/); [Lisa Lisson](https://lisalisson.com/what-genealogists-ought-to-know-about-photo-organization/)). Use the photos to open recorded oral-history conversations ([Smithsonian NMAAHC](https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/capturing-your-familys-oral-history)), interview the oldest relatives first, and ask about events that anchor a date—moves, cars, weddings, babies—rather than "What year was this?"

### Keep three verified copies, and store the prints cool and dry

Keep three copies of everything on at least two kinds of storage, with one copy offsite, and move them to new storage every five to seven years ([Recollection Wisconsin](https://recollectionwisconsin.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/321Plan_RWDI_2024.pdf)). Checksums are digital fingerprints that catch files that quietly corrupt, which backups alone can miss ([DPC](https://www.dpconline.org/handbook/technical-solutions-and-tools/fixity-and-checksums)), so create a checksum list for each box when you finish it, using a free tool such as BagIt ([Orbis Cascade](https://orbiscascadeulc.github.io/digprezsteps/fixity.html)), and check every copy once a year. Budget space realistically: 10,000 prints at 600 ppi come to roughly 260 GB as uncompressed 8-bit TIFFs, and a website that holds only web-size copies is not a backup. Backblaze B2 storage costs $6.95 per TB per month, so an offsite copy of 250 GB costs under $2 a month ([B2](https://www.backblaze.com/cloud-storage/pricing)); for a small "best of" set meant to outlast everyone, the nonprofit Permanent.org charges a one-time $10 per GB and uses an endowment to pay for storage indefinitely ([Permanent](https://desk.zoho.com/portal/permanent/en/kb/articles/how-our-storage-fees-work)). Return the prints to sleeves and boxes that pass the Photographic Activity Test—polyester, polyethylene or polypropylene, never PVC or vinyl—and keep relative humidity at 30–50%, because chemical decay roughly doubles with every 10°F rise in temperature ([NEDCC](https://www.nedcc.org/free-resources/preservation-leaflets/5.-photographs/5.3-care-of-photographs)). Store them in living space at about 70°F, away from attics, basements and outside walls ([NEDCC family guide](https://www.nedcc.org/assets/media/documents/Preserving%20Family%20Collections%202021.pdf)).

### Send analog tapes to a specialist, keep lossless masters, then cut chapters

Match the method to the format. MiniDV and Digital8 tapes are already digital, so transfer them over FireWire as the original DV files with no re-compression, though on a Mac the chain of adapters is fragile and Apple's FireWire adapter appears to be discontinued ([VintageCamcorder](https://vintagecamcorder.com/capture-minidv-to-modern-mac-2026); [Apple Community](https://discussions.apple.com/thread/255423558)). Analog tape (VHS, VHS-C, Hi8, Video8) needs a tape deck with a time-base corrector, a circuit that steadies the shaky signal; cheap capture sticks lack one, so the jitter gets baked into the file ([Each Moment](https://www.eachmoment.co.uk/blog/improve-old-video-quality)), and unless you want to hunt down that hardware, use a service. Film should be scanned frame by frame and kept at its original speed of 16, 18 or 24 frames per second ([Wikipedia: Super 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_8_film)).

Costco, CVS, Walmart and Sam's Club now send their transfers to Capture, formerly YesVideo, according to Capture and to a competitor writing in August 2026 ([Capture blog](https://www.capture.com/blog/costco-video-transfer-alternative); [Legacy Digital](https://legacydigital.net/versus/legacy-digital-vs-big-box-stores/)). iMemories lists $29.99 per tape, often discounted to $14.99 ([Pixoneye](https://pixoneye.com/imemories-cost/)), and Legacybox works out to about $31–35 per item ([Legacy Digital](https://legacydigital.net/imemories-vs-legacybox/)). Ask any service whether it delivers a lossless or high-quality master file or only MP4, whether the files are still interlaced and at what resolution (one comparison notes Legacybox publishes a 480p standard for film — [Zoopy](https://www.zoopy.com/imemories-vs-legacybox/)), whether it splits tapes into scenes, and whether the originals come back; avoid services that deliver only DVDs. The archival master is a lossless FFV1 file in an MKV container, still interlaced ([vhs-decode](https://github.com/oyvindln/vhs-decode/wiki/Digital-Tape-Guide); [One Bright Guide](https://onebrightguide.com/vhs-for-preservation-which-format-should-you-keep/)), and the viewing copies are standard H.264 MP4 files with the interlacing removed by a tool such as QTGMC or BWDIF ([vhs-decode wiki](https://github.com/oyvindln/vhs-decode/wiki/Standards-of-Public-Archives/21d972d66e74de2e2e392e40f5f39d1c5aa905a9)). Then cut each tape into event chapters such as "Christmas 1984 at the farm," give each chapter its own best-guess date, people and place under the same exact-or-estimated scheme as the photos, and keep the whole-tape master untouched. Video files don't use EXIF, so store each clip's details in a sidecar file following the IPTC Video Metadata Hub standard ([IPTC](https://www.iptc.org/std/videometadatahub/userguide/)) as well as in the site's database.

## Conclusion

The most expensive part of a family archive turns out to be its details, not its hosting. The cheapest option to run, Option 1's custom static site at a few dollars a month, is also the most engaging, while the decision that matters most—how to record a date like "summer, probably 1965"—has to be made at the scanner, long before any website exists. That reverses the usual order of work. The spreadsheet, the file IDs and the two-date convention are the real product, and the custom site, Immich and a family tree are interchangeable ways of viewing it. The Arant collection then becomes a second ID prefix and a second timeline track rather than a second website, and the website itself can be rebuilt from the files whenever technology moves on.

The other lesson is that the real competition for relatives' attention is the digital photo frame and the group chat, not another website. Institutions that succeed with crowdsourcing go to their volunteers with small, regular requests, like ETH's weekly mystery photos, rather than waiting for visits. The archive most likely to outlast everyone pairs a folder of well-tagged TIFFs with something on paper: a printed reunion book whose QR codes point to the family's own domain, so the links outlive any vendor ([Remento](https://www.remento.co/journal/the-best-memory-book-for-family-stories)). Build for regular nudges and for paper, and the website becomes the place relatives go when a photo on the TV or in a weekly email makes them want to know more.
